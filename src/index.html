When Outdated Technology Gets in the Way of Helping

Across Atlanta, nonprofits run food programs, youth clubs, shelters, and services for families going through tough times. They do a lot to support our communities but many are still working with outdated technology. Most can't afford to hire tech staff or invest in new software. On top of that, they're often expected to put every dollar toward helping people directly rather than improving the systems they use (Mao, 2026). One study that interviewed 20 nonprofits found that they still use technology much less than private companies, even when it could help them do their jobs better (Godefroid et al., 2024). As a result, staff spend more time dealing with paperwork and less time helping the people who need them.

The tech gap affects the people nonprofits serve

Better technology can help nonprofits reach more people and spend less time on administrative work. A study of more than 8,400 nonprofits found that organizations investing more in IT brought in more program income and devoted a larger share of their budgets to their missions (Mao, 2026). The problem is that many nonprofits simply can't afford to invest in better tools. In a 2025 survey of more than 350 nonprofit leaders, nearly 9 in 10 said budget was their biggest barrier. In Atlanta, where income inequality is higher than in any other large U.S. city, the effects can be especially serious for people who rely on these organizations for support (Jackson, 2022).

Source: The Chronicle of Philanthropy & Clarion Research (2025).

You have skills that can make a difference

That same survey found that having just one person who understands technology can make a big difference for a small nonprofit. Georgia Tech students already have skills these organizations need, whether it's building apps, designing websites, organizing data, or managing projects. You don't have to wait until you graduate to put those skills to use. You can start helping your community now.

Georgia Tech students aren't the first to identify this issue. Across North America, student teams are already working to close the gap. Meet Hack4Impact.

Hack4Impact

Hack4Impact was founded at the University of Pennsylvania in 2014 with a simple idea: nonprofits that can't afford developers can find them on campus. It is now a nonprofit network of student-run organizations at many universities across North America (Hack4Impact, n.d.-a). A local nonprofit organization applies with a project proposal, and a chapter team works with it for a couple of semesters to design and build the software. The nonprofit owns the delivered result for free, and students gain real project experience and exposure in return (Hack4Impact, n.d.-b).

    One of the largest chapters in the nation is right here in Atlanta. Meet Georgia Tech’s Bits of Good.

    Bits of Good at Georgia Tech

    Bits of Good is Georgia Tech’s Hack4Impact chapter. It started in 2017, when a few students revived a fading campus group called Humanitech to connect students with Atlanta nonprofits. That summer, the new club partnered with Hack4Impact (Bits of Good, n.d.-a). Today it has about 100 members (Hack4Impact, n.d.-c), and its student teams build web and mobile apps for nonprofits, many of them right here in Atlanta. Click for more information.

      What Bits of Good does

      Each semester, small teams of developers, designers and managers pair with one nonprofit partner and build a tool around its needs, from volunteer management to donation platforms. A dedicated maintenance team keeps past projects running, so partners aren’t left with software nobody supports after students graduate or move on.

      In order to keep everything running, Bits of Good has a wide range of roles students can get involved in.

      Roles

      Bootcamper

      New to Bits of Good? Bootcamp teaches the basics of web development or product design through hands-on projects, preparing you to join a real nonprofit project team next semester.

      Bootcamp Instructor

      Experienced members lead bootcamp sessions, teaching development or design fundamentals, reviewing work and mentoring new members as they build their first projects.

      Engineering

      Developers build web applications for nonprofit partners. Engineering managers lead each team’s technical work, plan sprints, review code and make sure the finished software is reliable and maintainable.

      Product

      Product managers are the bridge between students and nonprofits. They gather requirements, write roadmaps, keep projects on schedule and make sure the team builds what the partner actually needs.

      Design

      Product designers research users and create wireframes and interfaces for each project. Brand designers shape Bits of Good’s own visual identity across the website, social media and events.

      Nonprofit Partner

      Recruit new nonprofit partners, interview them about their challenges, and turn those needs into software proposals.

      Marketing

      The marketing team tells Bits of Good’s story. They run social media, newsletters and recruitment campaigns, and highlight finished projects so more students and nonprofits get involved.

      Operations

      Operations keeps the organization running behind the scenes: planning events and showcases, managing recruitment logistics, handling finances and supporting members across every team.

      Coding projects aren’t the only way for students to use their skills to aid a nonprofit organization. Business knowledge is also essential.

      Epic Intentions

      Nonprofits need to know how to use their resources. A web app can organize information on a nonprofit’s finances, but it’s useless if the members don’t know how to use that information. Epic Intentions helps bridge that gap by not only providing custom apps but also developing business and marketing strategies for nonprofits.

      Who can get involved

      Any student is welcome, but Epic Intentions’ projects often require skills in business, UI/UX, and programming.

      How it complements Bits of Good

      Bits of Good and Epic Intentions provide custom software for nonprofits. However, Epic Intentions also helps with the business side of things.

      Consult Your Community

      Consult Your Community focuses entirely on solving business problems for nonprofits and local businesses. Members thoroughly examine the whole organization and give advice on every aspect from employee training to fundraising strategies. They also host volunteer events for their partners.

      Who can get involved

      Any student can join, and training is provided to ensure that each member has the commercial knowledge to become a consultant.

      How it complements Bits of Good

      Bits of Good provides the technological resources to a nonprofit while Consult Your Community figures out how that technology can best be used.

      SDG 8: Decent work and economic growth

      SDG 8 calls for “decent work for all” and for higher productivity through “technological upgrading and innovation” (United Nations, n.d.-a). Initiatives like Bits of Good, Epic Intentions, and Consult Your Community help with both. They give nonprofits custom tools and business advice, so the same staff and volunteers can get more done. Consult Your Community also works with local businesses, and helping small businesses grow is another SDG 8 target. The students gain from actively participating in these initiatives too. They work with real clients, on real deadlines, as part of a team. That’s practice for their future careers, and SDG 8 specifically calls for decent work for young people (United Nations, n.d.-a).

      SDG 11: Sustainable cities and communities

      SDG 11 calls for cities that are “inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable,” and one of its targets is access for all to “adequate, safe and affordable housing and basic services” (United Nations, n.d.-b). Many nonprofits provide those kinds of services, so helping them run well is a direct way to make Atlanta more inclusive. Student initiatives can also make that help last. The students live in Atlanta, and the clubs keep going after members graduate, so a nonprofit can come back for updates or a new project (Hack4Impact, n.d.-b). That kind of steady, local support helps a community stay resilient. Some projects even help with sustainability directly. Bits of Good built Southface Energy Institute an online planner that makes sustainability standards easier to understand and use (Bits of Good, n.d.-l).
